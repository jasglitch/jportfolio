The thesis

A coach that is honest about what it does not know is more useful than one that is confidently wrong. Uncertainty should change how big an action is, not whether the coach acts at all.

01

The gap

Personal coaching works. It is priced out of reach for almost everyone.

  • A decent human trainer in India costs Rs 8,000 to Rs 25,000 a month.
  • A free plan from a PDF or a YouTube channel gets abandoned inside three weeks.
  • Almost every person who wants to train sits in the gap between those two.

The bet is that a coach reading real signals, adapting weekly, and explaining itself delivers most of a human coach's value at a fraction of the price. Not all of it. Enough to change behaviour for a very large group.

I scoped it as three tiers of one engine: someone who wants to start and not quit, someone holding a routine, and someone training for a race. Different data, different horizons, different levels of autonomy handed to the coach. If a tier needed its own logic branch rather than its own configuration, the architecture would be wrong.

02

What everybody builds, and why it does not work

The obvious version is a chatbot that generates a plan. It demos well and it fails quietly, because generating a plan is the easy half. Anyone can produce a reasonable twelve-week programme. Almost nobody adapts it well when the user misses two weeks, sleeps badly, or turns up with a sore knee and twenty minutes.

So the first real decision was where the intelligence belongs.

Stays deterministicNeeds an agent
Training load maths, overload ratiosInterpreting "my knee felt weird and I was up late with my kid"
Zone boundaries, periodisation, taper timingResolving signals that contradict each other
Calorie and macro arithmeticNegotiating the version of today's session the user will actually do
Safety ceilings and deload triggersDeciding when not to change anything

Physiology has known formulas. A language model computing them produces confident wrong numbers, which is the worst possible failure in a product people trust with their bodies. So: a deterministic physiology core, with the agent at the edges. The agent perceives, interprets, decides what to change and how to say it. The engine computes what the change actually is.

03

The problem nobody wants to look at

The coach has to make decisions from data of wildly uneven quality, and it gets worse over time.

A race athlete has a chest strap, a power meter and a recent lab test. A mass-market user has a phone step count and whatever they typed into a form three weeks ago. And everyone drifts: nobody cancels, they just stop wearing the watch.

Most fitness apps handle this by ignoring it. A weight typed in a month ago is treated exactly like a measurement taken this morning, and the output sounds equally confident either way. That is how these products lose trust. Not by being wrong occasionally, but by being equally confident when they are wrong.

So I built a model for it.

04

Authority scales with confidence

Every input gets a score: source quality, decayed by how old it is, gated on whether it is physiologically plausible. A lab measurement from last year is not a lab-grade input today.

Those roll up into one number per decision, and that number decides not whether the coach acts but how much authority it has.

Act0.80 + Applies the change and tells the user what it did and why.
Propose0.60 – 0.79 Queues it as a pending default. One tap to accept or skip.
Ask0.40 – 0.59 Holds the plan and asks one targeted question. Not a questionnaire.
Holdbelow 0.40 Changes nothing. Names the single input that would unlock more.

Four bands. A second, independent gate controls how big the change can be: 0.50 for a small step, 0.65 to change the week's structure, 0.80 for a deload or a block change.

Two rules that came out of this and surprised me

Self-report is not weak data. It is weak for measuring the body and it is the only valid source for everything else. No device measures whether someone has twenty minutes on Thursday, how sore their shoulder is, or what they are actually training for. Downgrading self-report across the board would have made the coach worse while looking more rigorous.

Missing data should cost you something without stopping the product. An input below the floor is dropped and its weight redistributes, then a coverage penalty applies. In practice a user with no wearable progresses in five percent steps instead of ten, and cannot add a session until they connect something. That is a better answer than either blocking them or pretending the gap does not exist.

05

The rule I would defend hardest

The safety asymmetry

Any change that reduces load, adds rest, or routes to a professional has no confidence floor at all.

The coach never needs to be certain to make training easier. It only needs to be certain to make it harder.

Uncertainty should always resolve toward the safer action. The cost of an unnecessary rest day is a slightly slower week. The cost of a missed overtraining signal is an injury and a churned user. Those are not symmetric, so the gates should not be either.

This is what fires in the opening example. The maths says hold. The action reduces load, so the floor does not apply, and the coach acts and names the disagreement out loud rather than hiding it. That last part matters as much as the decision: a human coach says exactly this, and it is what makes the product feel like a coach rather than an algorithm.

06

Then I simulated it, and the model broke

The spec was internally coherent and completely unshippable. That only became visible after running it thirty-six times.

I built a working simulator rather than a clickable prototype, because the thing I needed to prove happens across weeks, not screens. Three personas, twelve weeks each, every decision computed by the real model.

The coach never progressed anyone. All three personas sat permanently in Hold. Three separate causes:

What I wrote coherence = |Σ w·direction|

Divides by total weight, so a metric with no opinion counts against agreement. Adherence saying "push" while recovery sits neutral scored 0.40 when nothing disagreed with it.

What it had to be coherence = |net| / opinionated weight

A neutral signal is not a dissenting one. Most metrics have no opinion most of the time, and the coach should not be punished for that.

Correction one. The other two: I had written graceful fallback for missing inputs into the model and never implemented it, which structurally locked the entire mass-market segment out of ever progressing. And fatigue was measured against a fixed constant, so a six-session athlete read as permanently overtrained and sat in an endless deload.

I also changed how the coach chooses an action. It now proposes the largest step its confidence supports, rather than picking something ambitious and then failing its own gate. Same evidence, more confidence, bigger step.

What I take from this

A specification you have not simulated is a hypothesis. Three of the errors were invisible on the page and obvious the moment the model had to produce twelve consecutive weeks of behaviour for a person who was not cooperating.

The app on the left, the engine's reasoning on the right. The inspector exists so a reviewer can disagree with the coach and point at the number that was wrong.

07

One critique added an entire layer

Reviewing the build, the reaction was that the coach felt dead. It was right. My loop ran weekly, which is the correct cadence for changing someone's training and the wrong cadence for keeping them in the product. Six days out of seven the app had nothing to say.

The obvious fix is to say something every day. That is worse. A single day of steps tells you almost nothing, HRV has a three-day half-life, and manufacturing a confident daily insight from thin data is exactly what the whole confidence model exists to prevent.

The resolution was to separate what the two loops are allowed to do.

Daily cardWeekly decision
RunsEvery dayDay 7
DoesObserves yesterday, suggests one small action for todayChanges the plan
Changes loadNeverYes
Confidence gateNone requiredFull gate
PurposeA reason to open the appA plan that fits the person

The daily card can run on weak data because it never alters the plan. Six rules fire in priority order by what the data supports, and the most important of them is the one that fires when nothing is readable: the card says "I am working blind on recovery" and still gives the user one thing to do. It does not invent an observation to fill the space.

Every card carries one specific, today-sized action with a reason. After a miss: do the first ten minutes, you can stop after. On a session day: put a start time in your calendar. Never "get fitter", never a target weeks away.

With one exception. An athlete with a race date is motivated by the horizon, not despite it, so Tier 3 gets a visible countdown above the daily card. Tier 1 does not, because a visible distant goal is precisely what discourages someone who has already failed at one. Same engine, different configuration.

08

What I would watch

The dashboard is the other half of the build: what the company sees, against what the model assumed.

The metric that proves the whole thing works

Adaptation acceptance rate rising over time for the same user, with completion rate holding or rising. If the coach's suggestions get more accepted the longer someone uses it, the learning loop is real. If it stays flat, the personalisation is cosmetic.

Simulated cohort, 1,240 users, 12 weeks. I wrote the numbers to tell an honest story rather than a flattering one.

Three findings I built in because they are the ones this product would genuinely surface:

  • Coverage decay drives everything. Tier 1 loses its data source fastest, and Tier 1 is the volume segment.
  • The Act band is over-confident. It fires at 0.80 and lands 74 percent of the time. The threshold is set too low.
  • 31 percent of daily cards have nothing physiological to read, against 12 percent expected. That single number decides whether the mass tier is a real confidence-driven experience or a simpler product wearing the same skin.
09

What is unfinished, and what I got wrong

Open

  • The half-lives are reasoned, not measured. Three days for HRV, seven for resting heart rate. They come from how quickly each measure is understood to change physiologically, not from this product's own data. They are v1 defaults built to be retuned, and the dashboard is designed to tell me when to.
  • The daily card is an unproven retention bet. The argument for it is behavioural: small completed actions build consistency, and an empty app gets deleted. If daily engagement does not move week-four survival, the layer is cost without return.
  • Inference cost per user. A weekly agent run plus in-between triggers has a real bill attached. At mass-market pricing the margin is thin, and I have not modelled it.

Wrong

  • I wrote a rule into the model and did not implement it, and that omission would have locked out the segment the entire product exists for.
  • I designed one loop when the product needed two, and only caught it when someone said the coach felt dead.
  • I shipped a first version of the confidence formula that was elegant, defensible, and would never have let the coach do anything.

All three were caught by building the thing rather than by reading the document again.

Simulated throughout. Personas, session histories and cohort figures are generated by a deterministic model so the adaptation loop can be demonstrated end to end. No real users, no real usage data, and nothing here is medical advice.
